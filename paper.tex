\begin{abstract}
Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) agents trained on deterministic Atari environments
routinely converge to memorized action sequences (fixed scripts that replay the same
buttons in the same order regardless of game state) rather than developing reactive,
ball-tracking policies. Across 123 controlled experiments spanning every major
environment-modification approach (sticky actions, entropy tuning, auxiliary
cursor-alignment objectives, dynamics randomization, adversarial obstacles, and frame
skipping), no model produced a genuinely reactive argmax on Atari Breakout.
We identify a fundamental confound underlying these failures: every diagnostic
in the standard RL toolbox measures the policy distribution, not the
argmax, so models routinely appear reactive while evaluating to deterministic scripts.
We introduce the \emph{split-watcher}, a diagnostic that measures the argmax
directly by running the same model on two different brick layouts with
independent per-side predictions, and show that it detects memorization where
distribution-level diagnostics fail. We then demonstrate that a compact dense
proximity reward, which rewards the paddle for horizontal
closeness to the ball during descent, produces the first verified reactive PPO
argmax on Atari Breakout. The model achieves 0/240 perfect transfers on the
split-watcher (100\% no-timing ALT retention), an intervention gradient AUC of 0.421,
and a stochastic best score of 216 on clean evaluation. The result replicates
across four independent training runs with different random seeds (0/270 total
perfect transfers). A fading variant that linearly decays the proximity scale
from 0.05 to 0.0 over training achieves 428 points (full brick clearance)
while remaining reactive. A scale sweep (0.025, 0.05, 0.10) confirms 0.05 as
the unambiguous optimum: lower scales produce memorized scripts, higher scales
overwhelm the game reward. Directly rewarding the desired behavior succeeds
where penalizing memorized behavior fails: PPO optimizes whatever is in the
reward function. Making ball-tracking explicitly rewarding, rather than trying
to make scripts non-viable through environment engineering, is what ultimately
works.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)~\cite{schulman2017ppo} is one of the most
widely used deep reinforcement learning algorithms, and the Arcade Learning
Environment (ALE)~\cite{bellemare2013ale} remains a standard benchmark, popularized
by the DQN agent~\cite{mnih2015human} which demonstrated human-level play across
dozens of Atari games. However,
a well-known issue with training on deterministic ALE games is \emph{policy
memorization}: rather than learning to react to visual input, the agent learns
a fixed action sequence (a ``script'') that exploits deterministic environment
dynamics to achieve a consistent score without any genuine perception of game
state~\cite{machado2018revisiting,zhang2018overfitting}.

The standard mitigation is sticky actions (repeat\_action\_probability=0.25),
proposed by Machado et al.~\cite{machado2018revisiting} and widely adopted as
part of the ALE evaluation protocol. However, Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2018overfitting}
showed that sticky actions do not prevent memorization in deep ConvNet-based RL
agents: convolutional networks are ``automatically robust'' to 25\% action noise,
and a memorized sequence survives with only minor score degradation.

This paper reports the results of a systematic investigation into policy
memorization in PPO agents on Atari Breakout. Over 123 controlled experiments
spanning every major proposed environment-modification mitigation, we found that
\emph{no} such approach produced a reactive argmax. Every model
that appeared reactive under standard diagnostics was revealed as a memorized
script when the argmax (rather than the policy distribution) was tested
directly. A 124th experiment, using a fundamentally different strategy (dense
behavioral reward instead of environment engineering), succeeded.

The contributions of this work are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item We identify a fundamental confound in the standard RL diagnostic
    toolkit: every commonly used metric measures the \emph{policy distribution},
    not the \emph{argmax action}, and distribution-level reactivity routinely
    coexists with argmax memorization.
    \item We introduce the \emph{split-watcher}, a simple diagnostic that
    measures argmax behavior directly by running the same model on two
    different initial states with independent per-side predictions.
    \item We show that a compact dense proximity reward (directly rewarding
    the paddle for being horizontally close to the descending ball) produces
    the first verified reactive PPO argmax on Atari Breakout, achieving
    0/240 perfect transfers on the split-watcher.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent \textbf{Experiment naming.} Throughout this paper, individual PPO
training runs are referenced by numbered labels (PPO\_1 through PPO\_132).
These represent controlled single-variable experiments conducted over the
course of this investigation. PPO\_1--106 explored sticky actions, entropy
tuning, reward clipping, and architectural variants. PPO\_107--117 tested
\textit{cursor wrappers} (auxiliary training objectives that shape the policy
distribution toward a target paddle position by adding a cursor-alignment term
to the loss, without changing the game reward). PPO\_118--123 tested brick
layout randomization and transfer to BeamRider, another Atari game. None of
these 123 experiments produced a reactive argmax. PPO\_124, the proximity-reward
model, is the focus of this paper. PPO\_127--128 tested proximity reward scale
values (0.025 and 0.10). PPO\_131--132b tested fading and step-down schedules
for phasing out the proximity reward. PPO\_124a--124c are multi-seed
replications of PPO\_124 with seeds 1241, 1242, and 1243.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Memorization in Deterministic Environments}

The problem of policy memorization in deterministic RL environments has been
recognized since the early days of deep RL on the ALE. Machado et
al.~\cite{machado2018revisiting} proposed sticky actions
(repeat\_action\_probability=0.25) as a standard mitigation and documented the
prevalence of deterministic exploitation across Atari games. However, Zhang et
al.~\cite{zhang2018overfitting} directly tested whether sticky actions prevent
memorization in DQN and other deep RL agents, finding that ``stochasticity could
neither prevent deep RL agents from serious overfitting nor detect overfitted
agents effectively.'' The present work independently confirms Zhang et al.'s
finding across PPO agents: every sticky-trained model collapsed to a
deterministic script when tested without sticky actions.

Subsequent work has explored various approaches to forcing reactive behavior.
Justesen et al.~\cite{justesen2018illuminating} showed that agents can exploit
deterministic patterns even in procedurally generated environments. Cobbe et
al.~\cite{cobbe2020procgen} demonstrated that Procgen's procedural generation
improves generalization but does not eliminate it. The Go-Explore
algorithm~\cite{ecoffet2021goexplore} achieved superhuman performance on Montezuma's
Revenge but did so through explicit state-space exploration, not reactive
policy learning.

\subsection{Dense Auxiliary Rewards}

Adding auxiliary rewards to guide learning has a long history
in RL. Ng, Harada, and Russell~\cite{ng1999shaping} showed that
\emph{potential-based} reward shaping preserves the optimal policy (a
desirable property when the goal is to accelerate convergence without changing
what the agent learns). In deep RL, auxiliary tasks and dense rewards have
been used to improve sample efficiency and final performance~\cite{jaderberg2017auxiliary}.
The present work uses an auxiliary reward for the opposite purpose: rather than
preserving the original optimum while accelerating learning, we deliberately add
a non-potential-based reward to \emph{change the optimum itself}, making
ball-tracking the locally reward-maximizing behavior during training rather
than trying to make scripts non-viable through environment engineering.

\subsection{Diagnosing Memorization}

Standard evaluation protocols for Atari RL rely on aggregate metrics (mean
episode reward, unique score count) that do not distinguish reactive behavior
from memorized scripts. The memorization check used in this project's earlier
work~\footnote{Full project history and methodology at
\url{https://github.com/mharrell/BreakoutBot}} counts unique deterministic
scores across evaluation episodes, but we found that score variance from
life-loss timing and ball-bounce stochasticity produces false MULTIPLE\_SCRIPTS
verdicts for confirmed-memorized models. The split-watcher introduced here is,
to our knowledge, the first diagnostic that directly measures argmax behavior
rather than properties of the policy distribution or score distribution.

\section{Method}

\subsection{The Split-Watcher Diagnostic}

The split-watcher runs the same trained model on two different Breakout layouts
simultaneously, making independent predictions for each side. The left side
presents a full brick wall; the right side presents an altered layout (half
the bricks cleared, or a different arrangement). Both sides use the same model
weights and the same argmax action selection.

Different brick layouts cause different ball
bounces. On the altered side, the ball hits different bricks at different
angles and arrives at the paddle from different positions. A reactive policy
that tracks the ball \emph{must} move differently on the two sides. A memorized
script produces identical action sequences regardless.

We compute the Pearson correlation of paddle X-positions between the two sides:

\begin{equation}
    \text{px\_corr} = \frac{\text{cov}(\mathbf{p}_{\text{full}},
    \mathbf{p}_{\text{alt}})}
    {\sigma_{\text{full}} \sigma_{\text{alt}}}
\end{equation}

A px\_corr $>$ 0.99 with ALT score $\approx$ FULL score is classified as a
\emph{perfect transfer}: definitive memorization, physically impossible for a
reactive policy.

We evaluate across three layout types:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RIGHT\_HALF}: bricks 0--17 cleared (right half of the wall)
    \item \textbf{LEFT\_HALF}: bricks 18--35 cleared (left half of the wall)
    \item \textbf{RANDOM\_50}: 50\% of bricks cleared at random
\end{itemize}

We also provide a \emph{no-timing variant} that removes the NoopResetEnv
wrapper (which adds 0--30 random NOOP frames at reset), eliminating timing
offsets as a confound. Both sides start at frame 0 identically; any divergence
comes purely from different brick layouts.

Full code for the split-watcher is available in the companion repository at
\url{https://github.com/mharrell/breakout-reactive-ppo}.

\subsection{Intervention Gradient}

As a complementary diagnostic, we developed the \emph{intervention gradient}
test. Mid-game, after the ball has been in play for sufficient time for the
policy to establish a trajectory, the ball's horizontal position (ALE RAM
address 99) is teleported by a displacement $\Delta x \in \{0, \pm8, \pm15,
\pm30, \pm45, \pm60\}$ pixels. We record whether the paddle's next action
reverses direction to follow the teleported ball. A memorized script ignores
the teleport and continues its fixed sequence. A reactive tracker reverses
direction to track the new ball position.

A center-hold dead script (a policy that holds the paddle at the center of
the screen regardless of ball position) serves as the calibration baseline.
The reversal rate is plotted against displacement magnitude to produce a
dose-response curve; the area under this curve (AUC) quantifies overall
reactivity.

\subsection{Proximity Reward Wrapper}

The proximity reward wrapper reads three ALE RAM addresses at each timestep:
paddle X (72), ball X (99), and ball Y (101). When the ball is descending
toward the paddle ($\text{ball\_y} > 100$), a bonus is computed:

\begin{equation}
    \text{bonus} = 0.05 \cdot \max\left(0, \; 1 - \frac{|\text{paddle\_x} -
    \text{ball\_x}|}{80}\right)
\end{equation}

The bonus ranges from 0.0 (paddle and ball separated by $\geq$80 pixels, half
the playfield width) to 0.05 (paddle centered directly under the ball). At
$\sim$2,000 descent frames per game, perfect tracking earns approximately 50
bonus points, roughly 5--10\% of a typical game score and equivalent to 7
yellow bricks. The scale is intentionally small: large enough to steer the
policy toward ball-tracking over the course of a game (thousands of frames per episode), small enough not to overwhelm the game's
natural reward signal (1.0--7.0 per brick).

The proximity bonus is applied \emph{during training only}.
Evaluation and memorization checks use clean Breakout (no wrapper), testing
whether the tracking behavior transfers to the original reward function.

Table~\ref{tab:split} shows the split-watcher results. Across 240 games (120
no-timing, 120 with NoopResetEnv), the model produced \textbf{zero perfect
transfers}. In the no-timing variant, ALT score retention was 100\%: the model
cleared every brick on every layout, every game. With NoopResetEnv, retention
dropped to 46--59\%. The 0--30 random NOOP frames added at reset corrupt the
initial frame stack (four stacked frames may span the serve, causing the model
to miss the ball's initial trajectory) and shift the episode's starting phase
relative to any serve-timing patterns learned during training. Whether the drop
reflects purely a perceptual artifact (frame-stack corruption) or partial
memorization of serve timing is unresolved; both mechanisms predict the observed
sensitivity. The improvement from 46\% to 59\% between checkpoints is
consistent with increasing robustness under either account.

The mean px\_corr of 0.94--0.96 is below the 0.99 perfect-transfer threshold
but still indicates substantial correlation between paddle trajectories on
different layouts. A fully reactive policy may retain nontrivial px\_corr
because the ball's gross left-right motion is similar across layouts during the
opening serve and early bounces; the action-level divergence (62--71\%) is the
more direct measure of behavioral adaptation. For comparison, memorized cursor-wrapper
models (PPO\_111--118) and two BeamRider-trained models (another Atari game
where the same distribution-vs-argmax confound was independently observed;
see Experiment Naming above) all produced at least one perfect transfer (px\_corr
$>$ 0.99) under the split-watcher. PPO\_124 is the first model in the
project's history to achieve zero perfect transfers across all test
conditions.

Table~\ref{tab:intervention} shows the dose-response curve. The $\Delta x=0$
condition is a control: the ball is ``teleported'' to its current position
(i.e., not moved), measuring the model's natural rate of paddle direction
changes during normal play. A reactive tracker changes direction 37.5\% of the
time simply because the ball moves; this is the baseline, not noise. The dead
baseline (center-hold script) never changes direction, producing 0.0\%
reversal at all magnitudes.

Relative to the $\Delta x=0$ baseline, the net intervention effect is strongest
at $\pm$15px (+22.5 percentage points above baseline), declines to
+12.5pp at $\pm$30px, and becomes negative at $\pm$45--60px. The model
reverses direction to follow moderate displacements but does not chase a ball
that appears in physically impossible positions. This is the signature of a
reactive policy. The dead baseline confirms that the test correctly identifies
non-reactive behavior at every magnitude.

The AUC of 0.421 is computed from raw reversal rates and should be interpreted
with the 37.5\% natural-reversal baseline in mind. Sample sizes are
approximately 16--20 moving teleports per magnitude (teleports where the paddle
was in motion pre-intervention); rates should be interpreted as exploratory
given the small denominators. Prior cursor models (PPO\_107--117)
showed 33--50\% reversal rates on the intervention gradient but were confirmed
memorized by the split-watcher; those models had reactive \emph{distributions}
but memorized \emph{argmax modes}.

\subsection{Memorization Check}

Table~\ref{tab:memcheck} shows the memorization check results (deterministic
inference on clean Breakout, no proximity reward). The check runs the model
deterministically for 20 games and classifies behavior by the number of
unique episode scores produced: SINGLE\_SCRIPT (1 unique score, definitive
memorization) or MULTIPLE\_SCRIPTS ($>$1 unique score, not definitive — score
variance from life-loss timing and ball-bounce stochasticity can produce
false positives). PPO\_124 is the first model in the project's history to
sustain MULTIPLE\_SCRIPTS under deterministic inference without
sticky action masking. Of the 13 checkpoints in the 14M--25M range, 10
produced multiple unique scores (Table~\ref{tab:memcheck} shows a
representative subset). Earlier models achieving MULTIPLE\_SCRIPTS
did so only with sticky actions enabled (a known confound: sticky noise
produces 8--14 unique scores from a dead policy). Under stochastic inference
(stochastic) on clean Breakout, the model achieves a best score of 216, the
highest recorded in this project without sticky masking.

The best scores in Table~\ref{tab:memcheck} (87--107) appear substantially lower

their results are not directly comparable at a given checkpoint. The 19.2M
checkpoint's memcheck result was not recorded; the model may oscillate between
reactive and script-like behavior on short timescales, or the discrepancy may
reflect the test-protocol difference alone.

\subsection{Training Dynamics: Scale, Schedule, and Duration}

\subsubsection{Scale Sensitivity}
\label{sec:scale}

To characterize the sensitivity of the proximity reward approach to the bonus
scale, we ran two additional 25M-step training runs from scratch: PPO\_127
(scale=0.10) and PPO\_128 (scale=0.025), alongside the original PPO\_124
(scale=0.05). Split-watcher results at 25M steps (no-timing variant, 10
games per run) are shown in Table~\ref{tab:scale}.

At scale=0.10 ($\sim$100pt bonus, $\sim$40\% of game reward), the dense
proximity signal overwhelms the sparse game reward: the model learns to park
under the ball but largely abandons brick-clearing, scoring only 250. At
scale=0.025 ($\sim$25pt bonus, $\sim$6\% of game reward), the tracking
gradient is too weak to compete with script convergence: the model produces a
395-point memorized script uniform across all layouts. Scale=0.05
($\sim$50pt bonus, $\sim$13\% of game reward) is the unambiguous optimum,
creating a training landscape where neither the tracking nor the scripting
basin dominates, enabling PPO to alternate between them and producing
reactive checkpoints with 62.9\% divergence.

\subsubsection{Fading and Step-Down Schedules}
\label{sec:fading}

The scale sweep established that a constant 0.05 scale produces reactive
checkpoints. But can we improve on constant scale by scheduling the proximity
reward to be strong early (to establish tracking) and weak or absent late (to
let game reward dominate)? We tested two schedules:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fading (PPO\_131):} Scale decays linearly from 0.05 to 0.0
    over 25M steps. The policy experiences a gradually diminishing proximity
    bonus, incentivizing it to internalize tracking behavior before the bonus
    vanishes.

    \item \textbf{Step-Down (PPO\_132a$\rightarrow$PPO\_132b):} 15M steps at
    scale=0.05 (PPO\_132a), then 10M steps at scale=0.0 (PPO\_132b). The
    proximity reward is abruptly removed at 15M, testing whether tracking
    behavior survives without reinforcement.
\end{itemize}

Table~\ref{tab:fading} shows the results. All three schedules produce reactive
argmax policies (0/30 perfect transfers), confirming that the proximity reward
mechanism---not any particular schedule---is the causal factor. However, the
schedules differ substantially in final performance:

\textbf{Fading is the best variant.} PPO\_131 achieves the highest scores (428,
full brick clearance on both FULL and ALT) with the strongest intervention
gradient (AUC 0.402) and near-zero px\_corr (0.025). The gradual phase-out
allows the policy to bake in tracking behavior during early training, then
optimize game reward in the late phase without the proximity bonus distorting
the objective. Per-frame analysis over 28,410 frames confirms 72.5\% tracking
(the paddle is closer to the teleported ALT ball than to its FULL-side
counterpart).

\textbf{Step-down works but underperforms.} PPO\_132b retains reactivity after
10M steps at scale=0.0 (px\_corr 0.150, 71\% tracking), proving that tracking
behavior survives without the proximity bonus. But scores are lower
(186--307) and tracking is weaker than fading. The abrupt removal appears to
destabilize the policy; the gradual fade avoids this.

\textbf{Fixed scale scores too low.} PPO\_132a at 15M tracks the ball
effectively (px\_corr $-$0.027, AUC 0.357) but scores only 85 points. The
proximity bonus is still active and the policy has not yet learned to
capitalize on its tracking behavior for brick-clearing. Fixed-scale models
need more training time to convert tracking into scoring; fading compresses
this timeline.

\subsubsection{Extended Training and Oscillation}
\label{sec:oscillation}

Extending PPO\_124 from 25M to 50M steps (PPO\_126, identical configuration)
revealed that the proximity reward creates a training landscape with two
competing local optima---a tracking optimum and a script optimum---that PPO
cycles between with a $\sim$10--15M step period. A 12-checkpoint no-timing
split-watcher curve (5M to 50M, 30 games per checkpoint) shows 0/360 total
perfect transfers and divergence cycling between 10\% (script-dominated) and
68\% (reactive). Key checkpoints: 19.2M (62.4\% divergence, 0/60 perfect
transfers), 40M (14.0\%, script-dominated), 47.4M (65.8\%, reactive but
layout-asymmetric---tracks on RIGHT\_HALF at px\_corr=0.33, scripts on
LEFT\_HALF at px\_corr=0.97), 50M (68.2\%, reactive across all layouts).

The oscillation has two practical implications. First,
\textbf{checkpoint selection is critical}: a model evaluated at 40M or 45M
appears memorized; at 47.4M or 50M it appears reactive. A single checkpoint at
an arbitrary step count is not informative. Second, the oscillation
demonstrates that \textbf{reactivity does not monotonically degrade} with
training---the model can re-enter a reactive phase after tens of millions of
steps in a script-dominated phase. The proximity reward keeps both basins
accessible.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Why Proximity Reward Works}

Every prior anti-memorization method in this project tried to change the
\emph{viability of scripts}: make the environment stochastic so scripts fail
(sticky actions, random ball perturbations), add penalties so repetitive
sequences are punished (entropy bonuses, trajectory entropy), or make the
environment geometrically complex (moving bumpers, brick randomization). PPO's
optimizer always found a script that survived the changes: timing-robust,
layout-conditioned, or noise-tolerant. The optimum was always a script; only
the shape changed.

Proximity reward changes \emph{the training-time optimization landscape}. A
center-hold script (the default PPO convergence point for Breakout) receives
incidental proximity bonus when the ball happens to pass near center. But a
reactive tracking policy receives the maximum bonus on every single descent
frame, earning 3--5$\times$ more proximity reward over a full game. During
early-to-mid training, the gradient toward ball-tracking is unambiguous and
strong: track the ball, get more reward. The proximity bonus makes tracking
the locally dominant behavior for tens of millions of steps.

However, extended training (PPO\_126, Section~\ref{sec:oscillation})
reveals that the combined game + proximity objective has two competing local
optima---a tracking basin and a scripting basin---that PPO oscillates
between with a $\sim$10--15M-step period. The policy does not permanently
regress to a script; it cycles between reactive phases (divergence up to
68\%) and script-dominated phases (divergence as low as 10\%). The
asymptotic optimum of the combined objective remains contested: neither
basin permanently dominates. Checkpoint selection during a reactive phase is
critical; the best policy may occur at 19.2M, 25M, 47.4M, or 50M steps,
depending on where in the oscillation cycle evaluation occurs.

The bonus is dense (every frame) while the game's natural reward (brick
breaks) is sparse (every few seconds). Dense rewards provide better gradients
for credit assignment. The model does not need to discover the multi-step
causal chain ``track ball $\rightarrow$ hit ball $\rightarrow$ ball breaks
brick $\rightarrow$ score.'' It is told directly: being near the ball is good.
And because the scale is small (0.05 vs. 1.0--7.0 per brick), it does not
overwhelm the game reward. The model still cares about breaking bricks; it
learns that the way to break bricks is to be under the ball when it comes down.

\subsection{The Distribution-vs-Argmax Confound}

A critical methodological finding of this work is that \emph{every} commonly
used RL diagnostic (memorization check via unique score count, intervention
probe via distribution shift after state perturbation, SCAD probe via mutual
information between action and state, brick layout test via score
retention) measures properties of the \emph{policy distribution}, not the
\emph{argmax action}. But evaluation uses the argmax. A model can maintain a
reactive-looking distribution (90\% LEFT when ball is left, 90\% RIGHT when
ball is right) while the argmax is always the same action regardless.

This was confirmed empirically: cursor-wrapper models (PPO\_107--117)
showed 33--50\% reversal rates on the intervention gradient and MULTIPLE\_SCRIPTS on
memcheck, but were definitively memorized under split-watcher verification
(perfect transfer on altered layouts). The intervention gradient correctly
measures that the distribution $P(a \mid s)$ changes when the ball position
changes. It does not measure whether $\argmax_a P(a \mid s)$ changes. For
models with high-confidence distributions (e.g., 90\% probability on the
top action), substantial probability mass can redistribute among the remaining
actions without ever crossing the argmax threshold.

The split-watcher is, to our knowledge, the only existing diagnostic that
directly measures argmax behavior by comparing paddle trajectories on different
initial states. We recommend it as a standard verification step for any claim
of reactive behavior in deterministic RL environments.

\subsection{Limitations}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Single game, RAM-dependent implementation.} This work
    demonstrates the proximity reward approach only on Atari Breakout. The
    principle (directly reward the behavior you want) should generalize, but
    the specific implementation reads privileged ALE RAM addresses
    (\texttt{ale.getRAM()[72]}, \texttt{[99]}, \texttt{[101]}) to obtain
    ball and paddle positions. This requires per-game reverse-engineering
    of RAM layouts and does not extend to games without documented RAM maps
    or to environments that do not expose ground-truth entity positions.
    Applying this approach to, e.g., Montezuma's Revenge would require an
    entirely different state-access mechanism.
    \item \textbf{Scale sensitivity resolved.} The scale of 0.05 was found to
    be the unambiguous optimum in a three-point sweep (0.025, 0.05, 0.10;
    Section~\ref{sec:scale}). Lower scales fail to prevent script convergence;
    higher scales overwhelm the game reward. The sweep establishes the
    existence of a sweet spot but does not characterize it at fine granularity.
    \item \textbf{Finite training window partially characterized.} The
    12-checkpoint oscillation curve (Section~\ref{sec:oscillation}) shows that
    the proximity reward creates competing tracking and scripting basins that
    PPO cycles between, rather than a single ``reactive window'' that
    eventually closes. The fading schedule (Section~\ref{sec:fading})
    partially addresses this by phasing out the bonus. Whether an
    indefinitely stable reactive policy is possible remains open.
    \item \textbf{Seed sensitivity resolved.} The multi-seed replication
    (Section~\ref{sec:replication}) confirms that the proximity reward produces
    reactive argmax policies across four independent training runs with
    different random seeds (0/270 total perfect transfers). The result is not
    seed-dependent.
    \item \textbf{No baselines.} No non-PPO agent (DQN, human, oracle
    ball-tracker) has been evaluated on the split-watcher. The ``0/240
    perfect transfers'' result lacks a reference distribution for what a
    known-reactive policy's px\_corr distribution looks like on these layouts.
    \item \textbf{No statistical significance testing.} Results are reported as
    raw counts and percentages without confidence intervals. The 0/240 perfect
    transfers result is qualitatively clear, but formal statistical treatment
    would strengthen the conclusions. Intervention gradient reversal rates
    are reported without per-magnitude sample sizes; these should be
    interpreted as exploratory.
    \item \textbf{No ALE sticky evaluation protocol.} The standard ALE
    evaluation protocol (sticky actions enabled during evaluation) was not
    used here, as sticky actions are a known memorization masker. Clean
    evaluation (no sticky actions) provides the most direct test of argmax
    behavior.
    \item \textbf{BrickClearWrapper bug in prior results.} An early version of
    the split-watcher contained a bug in \texttt{BrickClearWrapper}: the
    \texttt{reset()} method returned the pre-clear observation, causing both
    sides to see identical frame-1 observations. This bug was present in all
    split-watcher runs for PPO\_111--118 and BeamRider models, and was fixed
    before PPO\_124 testing. The prior models have not been re-tested with
    the fixed wrapper, so their perfect-transfer counts may be inflated.
    The qualitative conclusion that those models were memorized is
    independently supported by single-script memcheck scores and zero
    intervention reversal on center-hold dead baselines.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Future Work}

The proximity reward approach opens several directions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Generalization to other Atari games.} The mechanism
    (dense reward for spatial alignment with a game entity) is Breakout-specific
    but the principle (reward the desired behavior directly) should transfer.
    Space Invaders: reward horizontal alignment with enemies. BeamRider: reward
    being out of the line of fire. Pong: reward paddle-ball proximity (trivially,
    since the game already does).
    \item \textbf{Scale scheduling (partially answered).} A fading schedule
    (scale 0.05$\to$0.0, Section~\ref{sec:fading}) produces the best observed
    policy (PPO\_131: 428 points, AUC 0.402, px\_corr 0.025, 0/30 perfect
    transfers). The approach works, but the policy still oscillates between
    reactive and script-dominated phases. Whether an alternative schedule
    (curved decay, warm-restart, adaptive scale based on divergence) can
    stabilize reactivity indefinitely remains open.
    \item \textbf{Combination with auxiliary supervision.} Earlier experiments
    with auxiliary ball-position regression heads (PPO\_102 and PPO\_103)
    showed that NatureCNN can track the ball at 1.9px MAE when directly
    supervised, but the policy still memorized. Combining proximity reward
    (which shapes the policy) with auxiliary ball-position
    supervision (which shapes the features) might produce even stronger
    reactivity.
    \item \textbf{Formal theory.} Under what conditions does a dense auxiliary
    reward create a reactive phase during training, and can the reactive
    optimum be made stable? A theoretical treatment of the optimization
    landscape for deterministic MDPs with auxiliary behavioral rewards would
    be valuable.
\end{itemize}

%-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Conclusion}

After 132 controlled PPO experiments on Atari Breakout spanning every major
proposed environment-modification approach, we found that no such method
produced a genuinely reactive argmax. Every diagnostic in the standard
RL toolbox measures the policy distribution, not the argmax. This confound
led to false positive reactivity claims across multiple experimental
generations.

The fix was a compact dense proximity bonus: reward the paddle for horizontal
closeness to the ball during descent. This makes ball-tracking explicitly
rewarding during training, rather than trying to make scripts non-viable
through environment engineering. The approach replicates across four
independent seeds (0/270 total perfect transfers on the split-watcher) and
survives a three-point scale sweep that confirms 0.05 as the unambiguous
optimum. A fading schedule that linearly decays the proximity bonus over
training achieves the best observed policy: 428 points (full brick clearance),
intervention AUC 0.402, and near-zero px\_corr of 0.025.

Extended training reveals that the proximity reward creates competing
tracking and scripting basins that PPO oscillates between with a
$\sim$10--15M-step period, rather than a single reactive window that
monotonically degrades. Checkpoint selection is critical, but reactivity
can return after tens of millions of script-dominated steps. The fading
schedule partially stabilizes the reactive basin but does not eliminate the
oscillation.

PPO optimizes whatever is in the reward function. In a
deterministic environment, environment engineering alone changes which script is
optimal; adding a dense behavioral reward makes the desired reactive behavior
a competing local optimum during training. The proximity reward succeeds not
by making scripts impossible, but by making tracking equally rewarding.
Directly rewarding the behavior you want may succeed where penalizing
memorization has failed.
